Dental Implants

A dental implant replaces the root of a missing tooth with a small titanium post in the jawbone, which then supports a crown, a bridge or a denture.

What it is

An implant restoration has three parts: the implant (a titanium post placed in the bone), the abutment (a connector), and the crown that you see and chew with. Over a few months the bone grows against the titanium and holds it firmly, a process called osseointegration.

Implants can replace a single tooth, several teeth, or support a full set of teeth.

Who may need it

  • Someone missing one or more teeth.
  • A tooth that cannot be saved and needs to be removed.
  • Someone whose dentures feel loose or uncomfortable.
  • Someone who would rather not have healthy neighbouring teeth cut down for a bridge.

Implants need enough healthy bone and gum, and good oral hygiene. Smoking, poorly controlled diabetes, gum disease, radiotherapy to the jaw and some medicines raise the risk of problems, so please tell us about them. Implants are usually left until the jaw has finished growing.

How it is performed

  1. Assess and plan. An examination, your medical history, and a 3D CBCT scan show the amount of bone and where the nerves and sinuses lie, so the position can be planned precisely.
  2. Prepare, if needed. A tooth may need removing, or a bone graft or sinus lift may be needed, followed by healing time.
  3. Place the implant. Under local anaesthetic, the post is placed into the bone and the gum is closed over or around it.
  4. Heal. The bone typically takes about three to six months to bond with the implant, sometimes less. In some cases a temporary tooth can be fitted in the meantime.
  5. Restore. The abutment and a custom crown are fitted, matched to your other teeth.
  6. Maintain. Regular reviews and cleaning keep the area healthy.

Alternatives

  • Fixed bridge. No surgery and quicker, but neighbouring teeth must be cut down, and the bone under the gap is not stimulated. See crowns and bridges.
  • Removable denture. The least invasive and often the lowest cost, but less stable and less comfortable to chew with.
  • Resin-bonded bridge. Less cutting, but weaker.
  • Leaving the gap. Possible, but neighbouring teeth can drift and bone can shrink.

Risks and limitations

  • As with any surgery: pain, swelling, bruising, bleeding and infection.
  • An implant can fail to bond with the bone. This is uncommon, and more likely in smokers and people with poorly controlled diabetes.
  • Peri-implantitis, an infection and bone loss around the implant, can develop if cleaning is not thorough or if you miss check-ups.
  • Rarely, nearby teeth, nerves or the sinus can be affected. Careful 3D planning is designed to avoid this.
  • Bone grafts add time. Screws can loosen and porcelain can chip over the years.
  • In published studies, implants placed in healthy patients commonly stay in place for ten years or more in well over nine out of ten cases, but results depend on the person, the site and the care taken afterwards.

Recovery and aftercare

  • Numbness wears off in a few hours. Swelling usually peaks in two to three days, and bruising can appear. Cold packs and prescribed painkillers help.
  • Eat soft, cool food at first, avoid pressure on the area, and avoid smoking and alcohol.
  • Keep the site clean as advised. Rinse gently with warm salt water from the day after surgery.
  • Many people return to work within a day or two after a single implant, though you may prefer to rest.
  • Contact us if pain gets worse after the third day, if you have a fever, if bleeding does not stop, or if the implant feels loose.
  • After the crown is fitted, brush and clean between the teeth daily and attend your reviews for life.

This page is general information, not a diagnosis. What is right for you depends on an examination of your teeth, gums, bone and general health, and your dentist will go through the options with you before any treatment begins.
