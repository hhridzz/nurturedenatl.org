Digital Intraoral Scanning & Diagnostics

Digital scanners and 3D imaging let us see your teeth, bone and gums in detail, so treatment can be planned precisely before it begins.

What it is

An intraoral scanner is a small handheld wand with a camera. It builds a detailed 3D model of your teeth and gums in minutes, replacing the trays and putty of a traditional impression.

CBCT (cone beam computed tomography) is a 3D X-ray scan. In one rotation it shows your teeth, jawbones, nerve pathways and sinuses, which a flat 2D X-ray cannot.

Who may need it

  • Anyone having crowns, bridges or veneers made, or night guards and aligners.
  • Anyone planning a dental implant, where the bone and nerve positions must be known.
  • People who gag on impression trays.
  • Complex cases, such as impacted teeth, infections around roots, jaw joint concerns, or upper teeth close to the sinus.
  • Patients whose teeth need to be recorded now and compared later, for example to monitor wear.

How it is performed

  1. Scan. The wand is moved gently over your teeth and gums while the 3D model builds on screen. It usually takes a few minutes.
  2. Check. The model is checked for gaps, and you can see it with us on the screen.
  3. Use. The model is used to design a restoration or aligner, or sent to the laboratory.
  4. CBCT, when needed. You stay still while the scanner turns around your head, which typically takes well under a minute. The images are rebuilt into a 3D view that your dentist reviews.

A CBCT scan is only taken when a 3D view will change the plan.

Alternatives

  • Traditional impressions with trays and putty. They are reliable, though some people dislike them, and are sometimes preferred for particular cases.
  • 2D X-rays (bitewing, small-film and panoramic), which use a lower dose and are enough for many routine checks, such as looking for cavities.
  • Medical CT scans, which are used only rarely in dentistry and give a higher dose.
  • Clinical examination alone, for simple cases.

Risks and limitations

  • CBCT uses ionising radiation. The dose depends on the settings and the size of the area scanned. It is generally higher than one small dental X-ray and usually much lower than a hospital CT. We use it only when the benefit justifies it. Please tell us if you are pregnant.
  • Scan accuracy can be affected by saliva, blood, a moving tongue or cheek, and deep edges below the gum. Digital scans are accurate enough for most single crowns, veneers and short bridges, and can be less reliable across a whole arch.
  • A scan supports an examination. It does not replace it, and some cases still need a traditional impression.

Recovery and aftercare

  • There is no recovery period. You can eat, drive and carry on with your day straight away.
  • Your jaw may feel slightly tired from keeping your mouth open.

This page is general information, not a diagnosis. What is right for you depends on an examination of your teeth, gums, bone and general health, and your dentist will go through the options with you before any treatment begins.
